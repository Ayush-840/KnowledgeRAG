Aurora Labs On-Call Incident Runbook
=====================================

This runbook describes how to respond to incidents in the Aurora Labs
production platform, which runs the Atlas analytics service, the Beacon
alerting service, and the Nimbus data pipeline.

1. SEVERITY LEVELS

SEV1: total service outage or data loss affecting customers. Respond within
5 minutes, page the full on-call rotation and the incident commander.
SEV2: significant degradation affecting a subset of customers. Respond within
15 minutes, page the primary on-call engineer.
SEV3: minor issue with a workaround available. Respond within 4 business hours.
SEV4: cosmetic or non-urgent. Log a ticket, no page required.

Severity is set by the incident commander and can be raised or lowered as the
situation evolves. When in doubt, start at the higher severity: it is always
easier to downgrade an incident than to recover from a late escalation.
Example SEV1 scenarios include a complete loss of the Atlas query engine, a
Beacon webhook outage affecting more than 25% of delivery, or corruption of
Nimbus pipeline state. A single customer's dashboard failing to render is
typically SEV3, and a misspelled label on the settings page is SEV4.

2. FIRST FIVE MINUTES

For any SEV1 or SEV2 incident, the responding engineer should follow the
"first five minutes" checklist: acknowledge the alert in the paging tool,
join the #incidents Slack channel, declare the incident with a descriptive
name, open a war room, and assign roles (incident commander, communications
lead, and scribe). Do not start debugging until the incident is declared and
roles are assigned.

The war room link is generated by the #incidents Slack channel and uses
Google Meet. The scribe captures a minute-by-minute timeline in the incident
thread, including every action taken, who took it, and the observed effect.
The timeline is the single most important artifact of the incident: it is
what the post-incident review is built from, so write it down as you go.

3. INCIDENT COMMAND STRUCTURE

The incident commander (IC) is the single decision-maker. The IC does not
debug; they coordinate. Their responsibilities are:

- Set and update the severity level.
- Assign tasks to engineers and track them against the timeline.
- Approve mitigations before they are applied to production.
- Decide when to escalate (see section 4).
- Run the post-incident review (see section 5).

The communications lead owns all external communication: status page
updates, customer messages, and the internal #incidents thread. The scribe
maintains the timeline. Everyone else in the war room focuses on the
technical investigation and proposes options to the IC rather than acting
unilaterally. A common failure mode is three engineers each applying
independent mitigations at the same time; the IC exists to prevent that.

4. COMMON FAILURE MODES

Nimbus pipeline backpressure: when the pipeline lag exceeds 30 minutes,
check the Kafka consumer group lag and the worker pool size. Scale out
workers by adding replicas to the nimbus-worker deployment.
Atlas query timeouts: when p95 query latency exceeds 2 seconds, check the
Atlas query engine connection pool. Raise max_connections to 200.
Beacon delivery delay: when alert delivery is delayed more than 10 minutes,
check the webhook queue and the rate limiter settings.

Additional failure modes seen in production:

- Atlas dashboard empty state: when a dashboard returns no data, verify the
  underlying query in the Atlas query log before assuming the pipeline is at
  fault. A common cause is a deleted table after a customer recreated their
  data source, which is not an outage.
- Beacon duplicate alerts: when customers report receiving the same alert
  more than once, check the deduplication key in the alert rule. Duplicates
  are usually caused by a rule whose deduplication window was changed while
  the rule was active.
- Nimbus schema drift: when the pipeline fails on a specific record type,
  inspect the schema registry for the topic. Roll back the schema change
  rather than skipping the records, and open a SEV3 ticket for the owning
  team.
- Authentication failures at the edge: when login fails for a subset of
  users, check the identity provider certificate rotation. Certificate
  expiry is the most common cause of intermittent 401 responses.

5. ESCALATION

If the incident is not mitigated within 60 minutes, escalate to the on-call
manager. If the incident affects a top-tier customer or lasts more than
4 hours, escalate to the VP of Engineering and open a customer
communication thread in the support portal.

Escalation is not a failure signal; it is part of the process. The escalation
path in order is: primary on-call engineer, secondary on-call engineer,
on-call manager, VP of Engineering, CEO for incidents exceeding 8 hours or
involving confirmed data loss. The IC should escalate early when the
technical investigation is stalled or when the incident touches more than one
service.

6. DATA LOSS AND RECOVERY

Any incident that may involve data loss is automatically SEV1. Do not modify
the affected systems until the recovery plan is agreed with the IC. Recovery
steps in order are:

1. Freeze writes to the affected service and capture a snapshot of the
   current state.
2. Identify the last known-good backup. Aurora Labs keeps hourly point-in-time
   backups for 30 days and daily backups for 12 months.
3. Restore to a staging environment first and validate row counts and
   checksums against the monitoring baseline.
4. Fail over to the restored data only after validation passes.
5. Communicate the recovery window to customers via the status page.

Never restore directly into production without staging validation. If the
backup itself is suspect, stop and involve the database engineering team
before proceeding.

7. SECURITY INCIDENTS

Security incidents follow a separate but parallel process. If you suspect
unauthorized access, credential compromise, or a data exfiltration attempt,
declare a security incident and page the security on-call via the
"sec-oncall" PagerDuty schedule immediately. Do not post credentials,
tokens, or suspected malicious payloads in the #incidents channel; use the
#security-confidential channel instead. Preserve logs and evidence: do not
rebuild the affected instances until the security team has taken forensic
copies. The security team leads the response; the IC supports them.

8. POST-INCIDENT REVIEW

Within 5 business days of mitigation, the incident commander must run a
post-incident review: write a timeline, identify contributing factors,
define corrective actions with owners and due dates, and file the review
under /incidents in the shared drive. SEV1 incidents additionally require an
executive summary approved by the VP of Engineering.

The review must answer five questions: what happened, why did it happen, why
was it not detected earlier, what will we do to prevent recurrence, and what
will we do to detect it faster next time. Corrective actions are tracked in
the engineering backlog with the "postmortem" label and are reviewed at the
weekly incident review meeting until closed.

9. COMMUNICATION TEMPLATES

Status updates to customers must include: what happened, what we are doing,
expected next update time. Updates go out every 60 minutes during an active
SEV1 incident.

Internal communication follows the same cadence in #incidents. The status
page must be updated within 15 minutes of declaring a SEV1 or SEV2 incident,
and the communications lead owns that update. For SEV1 incidents, the
executive team receives a summary every 2 hours through the on-call manager.

10. TESTING THE RUNBOOK

The runbook is exercised quarterly through a tabletop exercise and annually
through a full game day. Game days use synthetic incidents injected into the
staging environment, and the runbook is updated with any steps that were
missing or unclear. Every engineer is expected to have read this runbook
before their first on-call shift, and the on-call handbook references the
same procedures.

11. SERVICE-SPECIFIC QUICK REFERENCES

Atlas:
- Symptom: dashboard empty -> verify the query log before assuming outage.
- Symptom: p95 > 2s -> check connection pool, raise max_connections to 200.
- Symptom: login failures -> check identity provider certificate rotation.
- Rollback path: revert the last Calcite deployment via the deploy tool.

Beacon:
- Symptom: delayed delivery -> check webhook queue depth and rate limiter.
- Symptom: duplicate alerts -> check the rule's deduplication window.
- Symptom: no alerts at all -> verify the metrics source ingestion; Beacon
  silently downgrades to no-op when the source stream stops.
- Rollback path: disable the alert rule, then fix the rule, not the engine.

Nimbus:
- Symptom: lag > 30 min -> check consumer group lag, scale nimbus-worker.
- Symptom: schema failures -> check the schema registry, roll back the change.
- Symptom: sink errors -> verify sink credentials; S3 sink errors usually
  mean the bucket policy changed.
- Rollback path: pause the pipeline, fix, then resume; exactly-once
  semantics mean no events are lost during the pause.

12. INCIDENT SEVERITY EXAMPLES

SEV1: complete loss of the Atlas query engine for more than one region, a
Beacon webhook outage affecting more than 25% of deliveries, or confirmed
customer data loss. SEV2: Atlas p95 latency above the SLA target for more
than 30 minutes, pipeline lag above 30 minutes for more than 2 hours, or
a single-region partial outage. SEV3: a feature broken for a subset of
users with a workaround, a dashboard rendering incorrectly, or an alert
rule firing spuriously. SEV4: cosmetic issues and backlog tickets.

13. WAR ROOM ETIQUETTE

Keep the incident thread factual and chronological. One message per
meaningful action, with the actor, the action, and the observed effect.
Do not speculate in the thread; use a side channel for hypotheses that
have not been verified. Never post secrets, tokens, or customer data in
the thread. If you must share a token, use the secrets vault and share a
link. The scribe's timeline is built from the thread, so clean, factual
updates make the post-incident review faster and more accurate.

14. HANDOFF CHECKLIST

Before the next engineer takes over, confirm: the incident state and any
open mitigations are documented in the thread; the status page reflects
the current state; any temporary credentials created during the incident
are rotated or revoked; the handoff summary is posted in #incidents and
tags the incoming engineer; and open action items from the review are
assigned owners. A clean handoff takes 15 minutes and prevents the most
common cause of incident recurrence: the incoming engineer not knowing
what was already tried.

Contact details: on-call paging via PagerDuty schedule "prod-primary",
escalation via #incidents Slack channel.
